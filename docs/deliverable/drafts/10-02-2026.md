## **Executive Summary**

In this project, we model a proof-of-concept decision system for prioritizing tobacco retail compliance checks within the City of Pittsburgh. The implemented pipeline combines the Pennsylvania active tobacco-license universe, FDA undercover compliance-check history, Census tract data, school and youth-site proximity, and OpenStreetMap drive times. The active city universe contains 467 active licenses, including 392 retail licenses at 345 distinct physical locations; 188 retail licenses (48%) can be linked to prior FDA history.  
The analytical design follows a predict-then-optimize approach. A regularized risk model is trained on Pennsylvania undercover checks using only information available prior to each test period, calibrated to the Allegheny County Synar benchmark. In rolling FY2022–FY2025 statewide tests, the model achieved a mean AUC of 0.72 and top-10% lift of 1.47 (mean bootstrap interval 1.23–1.77), compared with a lift of 1.01 for random ranking. However, at the top of the list, a simple prior-violators-first rule performed similarly, indicating the model serves best as a decision and ranking aid rather than an absolute predictor.	  
On an operational level, the current Pittsburgh inspection volume is budget bound rather than route-time bound. That is, rather than *time* being an active constraint for execution, there is an apparent upper bound on how many inspections are executed *at all*. In routing sensitivity sweeps, a MILP route optimizer and a simple ranked-and-batched heuristic collected identical prizes at 4 to 8 monthly visits under normal day-length settings. Separate behavioral-response simulations indicate that deterministic top-store lists carry risks of gaming or adaptation, whereas rotating at-most-once and randomized policies remain stable. Consequently, we recommend a risk-ranked but randomized inspection list with a reserved random exploration share and rotation across retailers, backed by simple geographic batching at current capacity.

## **Analytical Model and Framework**

### *Problem Statement*	

Throughout the United States, the Food and Drug Administration uses tobacco retail inspections to deter and uncover tobacco-related violations among retailers, such as noncompliance with advertising regulations, selling single cigarettes, and selling to youth under 21 [(*FDA Program*, n.d.)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?lnS5UJ). These inspections are particularly effective at deterring retailers from selling to underage youth, further highlighting their preventive nature [(Abouk & Adams, 2017; Tangirala et al., 2006\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?OC0c4n).   
The FDA does not require a specific inspection sampling method, leaving implementation up to each state [(Lee et al., 2018\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?vKKDYy). Each year, Pennsylvania receives funding through the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to conduct about 10,000 tobacco retail compliance inspections through the Pennsylvania Department of Health (DOH) [(*FDA Program*, n.d.)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?v9JyUS). Throughout the state, the Pennsylvania DOH subcontracts with regional offices to perform inspections.   
While tobacco retail inspections are an important part of law enforcement, they can be costly, especially as public health budgets are limited [(Lee et al., 2018\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?tcT9Ob). Therefore, optimizing the sampling strategies for tobacco retail inspections would improve cost-effectiveness while maintaining the inspection’s ability to deter non-compliance. Such optimization would benefit the Pennsylvania Department of Health and the Federal Drug Administration, while tobacco retailers and other tobacco industry members may resist changes to existing processes that could increase the likelihood of uncovering violations and incurring fines.  
 Overall, this project focuses on determining the priority and schedule of tobacco retail inspections across all tobacco retail locations throughout the City of Pittsburgh. We use the City of Pittsburgh as a test case for the applicability of the prize-collecting traveling salesman problem in optimizing tobacco compliance.

## **Analytical Formulation**

### *Proposed Policy*

We suggest a modified version of the Prize-Collecting Traveling-Salesman Problem (PCTSP) in order to more intentionally manage Pittsburgh’s nicotine policy checks over random sampling. The Prize-Collecting TSP, in contrast to the traditional TSP, does not seek to reach all network nodes; rather, it seeks to maximize the total benefit (prizes) collected from the nodes accessed. Paul et al.’s “Budgeted” variation places further constraints on this model, forcing it to produce maximum benefit within a provided capacity. As enforcement infrastructure, staffing, and related monetary costs are all limited, we believe that a budgeted variant is most appropriate.  
Referencing findings in the environmental protection space (Harrington, 1988), regulatory agencies can still achieve high policy compliance through appropriate division of firms into “untargeted” and “targeted” regulatory groups. Firms are deemed risky and thus “targeted” based on prior noncompliance, whereas historically compliant firms are rewarded for good behavior with less severe scrutiny, becoming “untargeted.” Carrying Harrington’s findings to the tobacco regulation space, we assign tobacco sellers with higher potential negative externalities, greater time since past inspection, *and* prior noncompliance with higher risk scores. Risk scores become the collected “prizes”, minimizing the consequences of noncompliance.  
In order to characterize noncompliance as an externality-producing and thus quantifiable process, we draw on previous literature assessing the downstream healthcare costs associated with tobacco use. Tobacco usage has a well-documented correlation with negative health outcomes, including but not limited to carcinogenic impact, addictive behaviors, and declines in mental health. In lieu of per-store tobacco purchase volumes, we use proximity to vulnerable sites (schools, community centers, etc.) as a proxy for measuring externalities.  
We borrow conceptually from Stackelberg security games, as noncompliant retailers can be modeled as independent “attackers” under a nicotine regulation regime. In this view, regulatory agencies establish a “defensive” strategy in establishing a monthly schedule for compliance checks, whereas maliciously noncompliant sellers attempt to respond to this strategy. While we want to avoid introducing undue complexity, we also recognize that, given these games’ historical implementation in MILP form, conceptual elements are compatible with general model TSPs. Most importantly, we can characterize individual stores as acting from a perspective of *bounded rationality*: stores are aware they *can* be checked, but not necessarily that *they* will be. While we did not assign distinct “attacker” strategies in our proof-of-concept version of the policy (given low check rates in Pittsburgh overall), we were still able to characterize inspection teams as “defenders” committing to a given strategy.

### *Mathematical Representation*

Our problem can be characterized as a “predict-then-optimize” challenge. Each store $i$ is assigned a risk score based on prior violation history, proximity to sites of higher externality production (schools, community centers, etc.), and the recency of a tobacco sale compliance check. In cleaning the FDA history, because most inspection records lack usable Inspection Dates (813 of 911 city-limit records), Decision Date is used with a mandatory 30-day lag guard to ensure no future information leaks into the scheduler. Furthermore, entity resolution explicitly separates location identifiers (address \+ ZIP) from retailer identifiers (retailer name \+ location) to account for multiple distinct retailers operating at the same physical location over time. Then, our PCTSP attempts to maximize its prize collection while staying within budget. Our prediction and optimization strategy can be represented in its most generalized form as:

#### Risk Scoring

***ri*** **\= (*hi \* pi*) \+ δ*i***

* **ri**: The overall calculated risk score assigned to tobacco store *i*; positive, non-unit.  
* **hi**: The proximity measure to vulnerable locations (e.g., schools, youth centers) acting as an exposure proxy for negative externalities and negative wider impact  
* **pi**: A metric capture violation probability, constructed from a historical violation rate or prior noncompliance record for store *i*. It attempts to correct for sampling bias in which FDA records only reflect stores selected for inspection, rather than a true population.  
* **δi**: The recency metric of store *i*, growing in proportion to the days since the last agency check. A recently checked store is *down-weighted* in risk, but not eliminated. This element also actively penalizes a store *never* having been checked.

#### Objective Function (Prize Maximization)

Maximize collected prize, with a negligible travel-time tie-breaker (ε \= 10\-5 per minute):  
max  ∑i∈M ri yi

#### Where…

* ***M*** is the set of candidate retail locations (stores);  
* ***V*** **\= {0} ∪ *M*** is the set of all nodes, including the depot (0);  
* ***r*****i** is the calculated risk score (prize) of store ***i*** ∈ ***M***;  
* ***t*****i,j** is the travel time between nodes ***i*** and ***j***;  
* ***s*** is the on-site service time per inspection;  
* ***L*** is the daily route time capacity limit;  
* ***B*** is the total inspection budget;  
* ***x*****i,j \= 1** if the route drives directly from node ***i*** to ***j***, and 0 otherwise;  
* ***y*****i \= 1** if store ***i*** is inspected, and 0 otherwise.

#### Decision Variables

**xi,j** ∈ {0,1} (**i** ≠ **j** ∈ **V**): routing decision from node **i** to **j**  
**yi** ∈ {0,1} (**i** ∈ **M**): inspection decision for store **i**  
**ui** ∈ \[1, |**M**|\] (**i** ∈ **M**): position variable for subtour elimination

#### Constraints

∑j∈V xi,j \= yi   ∀ i ∈ M   **(2a)**  
∑i∈V xi,j \= yj   ∀ j ∈ M   **(2b)**  
∑j∈M x0j \= ∑i∈M xi0 \= 1   **(3)**  
ui \- uj \+ |M| xi,j ≤ |M| \- 1   ∀ i ≠ j ∈ M   **(4)**  
∑i∈V ∑j∈V, j≠i ti,j xi,j \+ s ∑i∈M yi ≤ L   **(5)**  
∑i∈M yi ≤ B   **(6)**

#### Element Mapping

| Element | Practical Role & Description |
| :---- | :---- |
| **(1)** | **Objective Function:** Maximizes the overall risk-weighted priority of inspected tobacco retail locations within operational and resource limits. |
| **(2a)–(2b)** | **Flow Conservation:** A store is entered and exited if and only if it is selected for inspection. |
| **(3)** | **Depot Flow:** Ensures the route starts and ends at the designated depot location. |
| **(4)** | **Subtour Elimination:** Miller-Tucker-Zemlin constraints to prevent isolated loops; that is, the suggested route must be continuous. |
| **(5)** | **Capacity Constraint:** Total travel time and on-site service time must fit within time limit ***L***. |
| **(6)** | **Budget Constraint:** Total number of inspections cannot exceed maximum budget ***B***. |

## **Analysis**

#### Empirical Assessment and Data Audit

	The empirical audit uses the available FDA Tobacco Compliance Outcomes export for underaged-purchaser-involved checks and restricts the city field to exactly “PITTSBURGH.” This removes records returned by the source search for places whose names merely contain Pittsburgh, including South, East, and West Pittsburgh. The resulting file contains 1,808 inspection-outcome records dated from May 31, 2011 through April 16, 2026\. After normalizing punctuation and whitespace in street addresses and combining address with ZIP code, the records correspond to 743 observed retail locations (746 distinct raw addresses-ZIP strings and 863 retailer name/address/ZIP combinations). Across these records, 208 checks recorded a sale to an underage purchaser, or 11.5 percent of observed checks. This percentage describes the inspected sample only; it is not an estimate of the violation rate among all Pittsburgh tobacco retailers.
